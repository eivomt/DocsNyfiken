Quantum Potential

A trajectory field generated from a regularized approximation of the Bohm quantum potential
Github link

This visualization presents a trajectory field generated from a regularized approximation of the Bohm quantum potential. The construction is inspired by Bohmian mechanics, an interpretation of quantum mechanics in which particles possess well-defined trajectories guided by the wave function.

The purpose of the visualization is not to reproduce an exact Bohmian system, but rather to illustrate how a complex interference pattern can generate a structured potential landscape and an associated force field that guides particle motion.

Bohmian Mechanics

In Bohmian mechanics, the state of a system is described not only by a wave function \Psi , but also by the actual positions of its particles. The wave function evolves according to Schrödinger's equation:

i\hbar\frac{\partial \Psi}{\partial t} = H \Psi

while particle trajectories are determined by the guiding equation:

\frac{dQ_k}{dt} = \frac{\hbar}{m_k}\operatorname{Im} \left[ \frac{\Psi^*\partial_k\Psi}{\Psi^*\Psi} \right] (Q_1,\ldots,Q_N)

The velocity field defined by the guiding equation determines how particles move through configuration space. The present visualization is based on a related force-based formulation involving the Bohm quantum potential.

Cylindrical Waves and Interference

The wave function used in the simulation is constructed from two cylindrical outgoing waves represented by Hankel functions of the first kind. These waves originate from two separated sources and serve as a simple model of the interference pattern produced in a double-slit experiment. 

        % Radial wavefunctions
        %Psi1 = besselh(0, 1, k*R1);
        %Psi2 = besselh(0, 1, k*R2);
        %Psi = Psi1 + Psi2;

MATLAB's besselh(nu,K,Z) function returns a Hankel function. For order zero, the Hankel function of the first kind is defined as:

H_0^{(1)}(x) = J_0(x) + iY_0(x)

where J_0(x) and Y_0(x) are Bessel functions of the first and second kind respectively.

A Hankel function of order zero is rotationally symmetric and therefore represents a circular wave propagating outward from a point source. Higher orders introduce angular structure, but only the zeroth-order function is required here.

The relationship between Bessel and Hankel functions is analogous to the relationship between standing and travelling waves. In much the same way that

\cos(x)+i\sin(x)=e^{ix}

represents a travelling wave, the Hankel function combines the two Bessel functions into an outward-propagating cylindrical wave.

Superposing two such waves with separated origins produces an interference pattern similar to that observed in a double-slit experiment.

Quantum Potential

In Bohmian mechanics, the force acting on a particle may be expressed as the sum of contributions from the classical potential and the quantum potential:

F_k = -\nabla_kV - \nabla_kQ

For a free particle, the classical potential vanishes and the particle dynamics are governed entirely by the quantum potential. Regions where the potential changes rapidly correspond to stronger forces, while flatter regions produce weaker accelerations.

Regularization of the Wave Sources

Hankel functions diverge at their origin, producing singularities that are problematic both numerically and visually. A simple cutoff removes the divergence but introduces discontinuities in the resulting surface.

Instead, the radial coordinate is replaced by an effective radius

R_{\mathrm{eff}} = \sqrt{R^2 + a^2}

which smoothly regularizes the singularity while preserving continuity of the wave function and its derivatives. 

        a = 8*max(hx,hy);
        R1eff = sqrt(R1.^2 + a^2);
        R2eff = sqrt(R2.^2 + a^2);
        Psi1 = besselh(0,1,k*R1eff);
        Psi2 = besselh(0,1,k*R2eff);
        Psi = Psi1 + Psi2;

Constructing the Quantum Potential

The quantum potential is computed from the probability density

\rho = |\Psi|^2

using finite-difference approximations implemented through five-point stencil convolution kernels.

Additional regularization is required because nodes of the wave function can produce extremely large values. Small denominators are protected by an epsilon parameter, and the resulting potential is clipped to a finite numerical range before further processing. 

        Rho = abs(Psi).^2;
        Qpot = conv2(Rho, Kernel_Gradient_X, 'same').^2;
        Qpot = Qpot + conv2(Rho, Kernel_Gradient_Y, 'same').^2;
        Qpot = -0.5*Qpot./(Rho+Epsilon).^2;
        Qpot = Qpot + conv2(Rho, Kernel_Laplace, 'same')./(Rho+Epsilon);
        Qpot = -1/4*Qpot;

        MinVal = -500;
        MaxVal = 10;

        Qpot = max(Qpot, MinVal);
        Qpot = min(Qpot, MaxVal);

A final smoothing pass is applied before the potential is stored and used in later stages of the simulation.

Constructing the Force Field

Once the quantum potential has been computed, its spatial gradient is evaluated. The force field used by the visualization is simply the negative gradient of the potential:

\mathbf{F} = -\nabla Q

The resulting force vectors are precomputed and stored as a lookup table. During the simulation, particle accelerations are obtained by interpolating this field rather than recomputing gradients in real time. 

        dQdx = conv2(Qpot, Kernel_Gradient_X, 'same');
        dQdy = conv2(Qpot, Kernel_Gradient_Y, 'same');

        Fx = -dQdx;
        Fy = -dQdy;

Visualization Pipeline

The quantity displayed in the browser is not the raw quantum potential. The exact potential contains sharp peaks and large variations that obscure its overall structure and make numerical visualization difficult.

To produce a more informative representation, the potential is smoothed and compressed using a hyperbolic tangent transformation: 

        Qmaximum = 1;
        Qscale = 50;
        Qvis_compressed = Qmaximum * tanh(Qpot / Qscale);

The displayed surface should therefore be interpreted as a visualization of the underlying structure rather than a direct rendering of the physical quantity itself. The force field driving the trajectories and the surface shown on screen are related, but are not identical datasets.




MATLAB Source

The complete MATLAB script used to generate the potential, force field, and visualization data is included below for reference. 

        % Parameters
        N = 1000;            % Grid size
        L = 10;              % Physical size (0 to L in x and y)
        k = 5;               % Wave number
        d = 7;               % Distance between centres
        Sp = 98.5;           % Smoothing

        % Create 2D grid
        x = linspace(-L, L, N);
        y = linspace(-L, L, N);
        [X, Y] = meshgrid(x, y);
        R1 = sqrt(X.^2 + (Y+d/2).^2);   % Radial distance from centre 1
        R2 = sqrt(X.^2 + (Y-d/2).^2);   % Radial distance from centre 1


        hx = x(2)-x(1);
        hy = y(2)-y(1);

        % Cutoff
        %Rmin = 2e-1;
        %R1(R1<Rmin) = Rmin;
        %R2(R2<Rmin) = Rmin;

        % Smooth core
        a = 8*max(hx,hy);
        R1eff = sqrt(R1.^2 + a^2);
        R2eff = sqrt(R2.^2 + a^2);
        Psi1 = besselh(0,1, k*R1eff);
        Psi2 = besselh(0,1, k*R2eff);
        Psi = Psi1 + Psi2;

        % Radial wavefunctions
        %Psi1 = besselh(0, 1, k*R1);   % J_0(k*r)
        %Psi2 = besselh(0, 1, k*R2);   % J_0(k*r)
        %Psi = Psi1 + Psi2;

        % For calculating, smoothening and regularizing the quantum potential
        Epsilon = 1e-6;                         % Used in regularization
        % Kernel for Laplacian (5 point stencil)
        wx = 1/(12*hx^2);
        wy = 1/(12*hy^2);
        Kernel_Laplace = [0 0 -wy 0 0; 
            0 0 16*wy 0 0;
            -wx 16*wx -30*(wx+wy) 16*wx -wx;
            0 0 16*wy 0 0;
            0 0 -wy 0 0]; 
        % Kernel for gradient (5 point stencil)
        Kernel_Gradient_X = [1 -8 0 8 -1]*1/(12*hx);
        Kernel_Gradient_Y = [1 -8 0 8 -1].'*1/(12*hy);
        % Kernel for Gaussian-like convolution
        NN = 4/(hx*hy) + 2*(1/hx + 1/hy) + 1;
        Kernel_Gauss = 1/NN*[1/(hx*hy) 1/hy 1/(hx*hy); 
        1/hx 1 1/hx; 1/(hx*hy) 1/hy 1/(hx*hy)];

        % Construct quantum potential
        Rho = abs(Psi).^2;
        Qpot = conv2(Rho, Kernel_Gradient_X, 'same').^2;
        Qpot = Qpot + conv2(Rho, Kernel_Gradient_Y, 'same').^2;
        Qpot = -0.5*Qpot./(Rho+Epsilon).^2;
        Qpot = Qpot + conv2(Rho, Kernel_Laplace, 'same')./(Rho+Epsilon);
        Qpot = -1/4*Qpot;
        MinVal = -500;
        MaxVal = 10;
        Qpot = max(Qpot, MinVal);
        Qpot = min(Qpot, MaxVal);

        % Smoothening by convolution
        Qpot = conv2(Qpot, Kernel_Gauss, 'same');

        writematrix(Qpot, '/Users/eo/Documents/code/Bohmian-Double-Slit-main/Qpot.csv');

        dQdx = conv2(Qpot, Kernel_Gradient_X, 'same'); 
        dQdy = conv2(Qpot, Kernel_Gradient_Y, 'same');

        Fx = -dQdx; 
        Fy = -dQdy;


        nb = 3;   % boundary thickness in grid points (>= stencil radius)


        Fx(1:nb,:)        = NaN;
        Fx(end-nb+1:end,:) = NaN;
        Fx(:,1:nb)        = NaN;
        Fx(:,end-nb+1:end) = NaN;

        Fy(1:nb,:)        = NaN;
        Fy(end-nb+1:end,:) = NaN;
        Fy(:,1:nb)        = NaN;
        Fy(:,end-nb+1:end) = NaN;
        
        dataForce = [X(:), Y(:), Fx(:), Fy(:)]; 
        writematrix(dataForce, '/Users/eo/Documents/code/Bohmian-Double-Slit-main/data/Q_gradient_negative.csv'); 



        % Interpolate at wave centers
        x0 = 0;
        y1 = -d/2;
        y2 = d/2;

        Q1_center = interp2(X, Y, Qpot, x0, y1, 'spline');
        Q2_center = interp2(X, Y, Qpot, x0, y2, 'spline');

        Q = Qpot;
        S = prctile(abs(Q(:)), Sp);
        QpotVisualize = S * tanh(Q / S);



        Fx = QpotVisualize;
        Fx(1:nb,:)        = NaN;
        Fx(end-nb+1:end,:) = NaN;
        Fx(:,1:nb)        = NaN;
        Fx(:,end-nb+1:end) = NaN;

        Qmaximum = 1;
        Qscale = 50;
        Qvis_compressed = Qmaximum * tanh(Fx / Qscale);


        % Cut off edges and peaks for visualization
        %QpotVisualize = Qpot.*(abs(X)<0.9*L).*(abs(Y)<0.9*L);
        %Qmax = 1e2;   % choose based on your system units
        %QpotVisualize = min(max(QpotVisualize, -Qmax), Qmax);

        % Remove elements within the cutoff
        %for Xind = 1:N
        %  for Yind = 1:N
        %    if R1(Xind, Yind) <= 1.1*Rmin | R2(Xind, Yind) <= 1.1*Rmin
        %      QpotVisualize(Xind,Yind) = NaN;
        %    end
        %  end
        %end

        cmap = [ ...
            linspace(0.97,0.90,100)', ...  % R: off-white → light tan
            linspace(0.96,0.92,100)', ...  % G
            linspace(0.90,0.85,100)'  ...  % B
        ];

        data = [X(:), Y(:), Qvis_compressed(:)];
        writematrix(data, '/Users/eo/Documents/code/Bohmian-Double-Slit-main/data/Q.csv');

        % Visualization
        figure(1);
        surf(X, Y, Qvis_compressed)
        shading interp
        axis equal
        colormap("summer")
        xlabel('x') 
        ylabel('y') 
        colorbar

        % Same - as colourplot
        figure(2);
        pcolor(X, Y, Qvis_compressed)
        shading interp
        colormap(cmap)
        axis square
        xlabel('x') 
        ylabel('y') 
        colorbar

        figure;

        % Filled background (subtle color wash)
        contourf(X, Y, Qvis_compressed, 100, 'LineColor', [.7 .7 .7]);
        axis equal tight
        hold on

        % Contour lines (the actual structure)
        contour(X, Y, Qvis_compressed, 99.99999999, ...
            'LineColor', [1.00 1.00 1.00], ...
            'LineWidth', 0.5);

        hold off

        colormap(cmap)
        colorbar
        xlabel('x')
        ylabel('y')
