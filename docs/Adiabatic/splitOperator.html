split operator method

unitaryStep() is run every animationFrame to get \Psi(x,t + \Delta t) from \Psi(x,t). It does so by implementing the split operator method, explained below.

                const PsiX1 = tf.spectral.ifft(PsiK2);

                let Psi1Im = tf.imag(PsiX1); 

                // ====== POTENTIAL + ABSORPTION FULL STEP ====== 
                const absorb = tf.exp(tf.mul(tf.scalar(-dt, 'float32'), GammaX));

time evolution

The time dependent Schrödinger equation:
i\hbar\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial t}=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial x^2} + V\psi
can be written in terms of the Hamiltionian \hat{H} which is the sum of the kinetic and potential energy operators \hat{H} = \hat{T} + \hat{V}.
The time dependent Schrödinger equation then becomes:
i\hbar\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial t}=\hat{H}\psi.
Inserting \hat{H} = \hat{T} + \hat{V} and rearranging i\hbar, we get:
\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial t}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}(\hat{T} + \hat{V})\psi
an expression for how psi changes in time, excactly what we need to update the wave function each timestep, and the expression has the solution:
\psi(t+dt)=e^{-i(\hat{T}+\hat{V})dt/\hbar}\psi(t) .
Since a^{x+y} = a^{x} a^{y} you could be forgiven for thinking we could split the exponent and get e^{-i(\hat{T}+\hat{V})dt/\hbar}\psi(t) = e^{-i(\hat{T})dt/\hbar} \; e^{-i(\hat{V})dt/\hbar}\psi(t) .
The potential and kinetic operators however, generally do not commute. This equality therefore, is not excact, so we approximate the solution using the split-operator method
e^{-i(\hat{T}+\hat{V})dt/\hbar} \approx e^{-i\hat{T}dt/(2\hbar)} e^{-i\hat{V}dt/\hbar} e^{-i\hat{T}dt/(2\hbar)} .
which has global error of order
dt^2 .
In our case we simplify the world just a little bit by setting \hbar = 1 so we are left with:

\psi(t+dt) \approx e^{-i\hat{T}dt/2} e^{-i\hat{V}dt} e^{-i\hat{T}dt/2} \; \psi(t) .

I feel very strongly that epic fanfares should be played whenever this expression is reached. This however, is not the end of the story, to implement this, hoops must be jumped through.

time domain / frequency domain

there and back again

applying the potential term to \psi(t) is straight forward. \hat{V}(x)\psi(x) = V(x)\psi(x) , that's just multiplication.
\psi(x) \to e^{-iV(x)\frac{dt}{\hbar}} \; \psi(x)
The kinetic operator on the other hand \hat{T} = -\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m} \; \frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial x^2}
involves derivatives, which makes applying the kinetic term to \psi(x) tricky. Luckily the Fourier transform provides a way to circumvent this. A Fourier transform decomposes any wavefunction into plane waves:
\psi(x) = \int \tilde{\psi}(k)e^{ikx}\, dk .
If the Fourier transform is new to you, i highly recommend this explanation:
But what is the Fourier Transform? A visual introduction.

plane waves are eigenfunctions of derivatives:
\nabla^2 e^{ikx} = -k^2e^{ikx}
Why that is was not intuitive to me so i'll provide the explanation.
After applying the Fourier transform psi is expressed in terms of plane waves of the form e^{ikx} , if we take the derivative of that plane wave we get:
\frac{d}{dx}e^{ikx} = ike^{ikx}
so e^{ikx} is an eigenfunction of the derivative operator with eigenvalue ik .
\frac{d}{dx} \psi = ik\psi, \quad \hat{O}\psi = \lambda\psi, \quad \lambda = ik
To get the second derivative we apply the derivative operator again and get
\lambda^2 \psi, \quad \lambda^2 = i^2k^2 = -1k^2 = -k^2
\nabla^2 e^{ikx} = -k^2e^{ikx}
If we use this to write the kinetic operator in momentum space, it now reads:
\hat{T} \to \frac{\hbar^{2}k^{2}}{2m}
This is once again just multiplication, applying the kinetic term to psi is therefore trivial, if we do so in momentum space. That is why, to implement the split operator method defined above, the algorithm goes:

  • FFT to momentum space
  • apply kinetic half step
  • IFFT back to position space
  • apply potential step
  • FFT to momentum space again
  • apply another kinetic half step
  • IFFT back to position space

At the end of which we have evolved our wavefunction in time.
\psi(x,t) \to \psi(x,t + \Delta t)
