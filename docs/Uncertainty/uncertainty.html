Uncertainty

have you ever applied a film to a surface and found theres a little air bubble in it so you try and press it down and it works so you're real happy before you notice the air bubble just appeared somewhere else. That's pretty much the uncertainty principle, there will always be an air bubble.

A visualization of Heisenbergs uncertainty principle showing the effect of the narrowing of knowledge of an observable on the knowledge of its complementary observable. In this case the chosen pair of complementary observables are position and momentum, a true classic.

Both properties cannot be known simultaneously:

\sigma_x \sigma_p \geq \frac{\hbar}{2}

Users can change the mean value of position and momentum and set \sigma_x , resulting in the respective graphs moving and changing shapes accordingly. The blue graph represents position while the red graph represents momentum. As \sigma_x is changed one becomes narrower while the other is more spread out. There's an upper and lower limit to \sigma_x , while \sigma_x and \sigma_p can in theory be larger or smaller than this (approaching 0 or infinity), that would allow the plots to be infinitely tall.

Setting \sigma_x indirectly sets \sigma_p , because we are plotting the maximum possible accuracy for both. 

        SigmaX = sigmaXSlider.value
        SigmaP = 1/(2 * SigmaX)

as always, we set \hbar = 1 to simplify:

\sigma_p = \frac{1}{2 \sigma_x}
\sigma_x \sigma_p = \frac{\sigma_x}{2 \sigma_x} = \frac{1}{2}
