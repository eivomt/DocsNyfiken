Uncertainty

The uncertainty principle is one of the central results of quantum mechanics. It states that certain pairs of physical observables cannot simultaneously be known with arbitrary precision. Improving the precision of one observable necessarily reduces the precision with which its complementary observable can be specified.

This visualization explores Heisenberg's uncertainty principle using the canonical pair of complementary observables: position and momentum. The relationship between their uncertainties is given by

\sigma_x \sigma_p \geq \frac{\hbar}{2}

The principle does not arise from limitations of measurement technology. Rather, it is a fundamental property of quantum systems and is closely connected to the fact that the position and momentum operators do not commute. As David Griffiths notes:

In a deep sense, all of the mysteries of quantum mechanics can be traced to the fact that position and momentum do not commute.

While the full significance of this statement extends well beyond the scope of this visualization, the uncertainty relation provides one of its most direct consequences.

Interaction

The user can adjust the mean position, mean momentum, and position uncertainty \sigma_x . The blue curve represents the probability distribution in position space, while the red curve represents the corresponding distribution in momentum space.

Changing the mean values shifts the distributions along their respective axes. Changing \sigma_x alters the widths of both distributions: as the position distribution becomes narrower, the momentum distribution becomes broader, and vice versa.

The visualization imposes upper and lower limits on \sigma_x for practical reasons. In principle, the uncertainties may become arbitrarily large or arbitrarily small, approaching infinity or zero. Such values would produce distributions that are impractical to display on a finite canvas.

Minimum-Uncertainty States

The visualization displays Gaussian wave packets that saturate the uncertainty bound. Consequently, changing \sigma_x automatically determines the corresponding momentum uncertainty \sigma_p . 

        SigmaX = sigmaXSlider.value
        SigmaP = 1/(2 * SigmaX)

Throughout the simulation we use natural units with \hbar = 1 . The uncertainty relation therefore becomes

\sigma_p = \frac{1}{2\sigma_x}
\sigma_x \sigma_p = \frac{\sigma_x}{2\sigma_x} = \frac{1}{2}

The displayed states therefore achieve the minimum uncertainty allowed by quantum mechanics. Any further reduction in one uncertainty necessarily increases the other.
